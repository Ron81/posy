What is transmitted, and why
DataTracker deliversAvatar can showTransmitted

A part is transmitted only if the tracker delivers it and the avatar can show it (spec §2.1). That set is declared once as caps.sends = . Everything else stays in its rest pose on the right and costs no bytes. The right-hand camera frames the declared region. The stick figure has a part for every data type: fingers, one toe piece per foot, lids, mouth, tongue and pupils. What a loaded model can show is read from the model. Finger joints are worked out on the receiving side from the finger block (spec §5.5); the face comes from the expression block (§5.6).

About the poses, hips height and send rate

Poses. Whole-body poses written as joint angles in the spec's conventions (§3.4). "Auto" cycles through them. The last group holds the conformance pose vectors from testvectors/poses, legs only.

Hands, feet and face. "Count to ten" raises one hand and opens it finger by finger, thumb first, then the other hand. "Toes" lifts the toes of each foot, then stands on tiptoe. "Face" goes through the lids, the mouth shapes, the tongue and the gaze one at a time; the step that is on is named next to the selector. The view selector moves both cameras close to the hands, the feet or the face; on "auto" these three poses do that themselves. On a model, the toes are easiest to see barefoot, and the tongue direction has nothing to drive: only the stick figure shows it.

What moves. In every pose the hands and the face do what fits it: hands hang relaxed, lie flat on the lap, grip the hips or open for a wave; the mouth moves when the pose speaks or shouts. Otherwise only the lids blink and the eyes follow the head.

Hips height. The sender estimates how high the hips are from the lowest point of the legs (spec Appendix D) and transmits it as h. On the high stool and in the air nothing touches the floor, so those poses supply the height themselves.

Send rate is how many pose frames leave the sender each second (FPS). Spec tiers: 30 FPS (FULL) looks smooth; 15 FPS (NORMAL) still looks fine because the receiver interpolates between frames; 5 FPS (MINIMAL) is the readable floor. (The spec calls this rate in Hz; it's the same number.)

Thanks to the projects this demo stands on

three.js — does all the 3D heavy lifting here: scenes, cameras, the WebGL renderer, and the quaternion maths we lean on when applying decoded rotations. It's the backbone of basically all 3D on the open web, and a from-scratch mocap tool like this simply wouldn't come together in a reasonable time without it. Thank you for keeping something this powerful free and readable.

@pixiv/three-vrm — makes VRM avatars first-class citizens in three.js: humanoid bone mapping, expressions, the whole rig. When you load your own .vrm above, this is what brings it to life. pixiv open-sourcing the VRM tooling is a big part of why indie VTubing is something a hobbyist can actually reach — genuinely grateful that it exists and that it's open.

And of course VRM itself, and the broader VTuber / mocap community whose formats and habits this protocol tries to fit into rather than fight.
